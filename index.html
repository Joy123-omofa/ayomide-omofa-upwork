What a line edit actually does to a paragraph.

Twelve real editorial decisions, each one shown three ways: the author's sentence, my edit, and the margin comment that explains it. Notice what stays. The wording is the author's in every case — the edit fixes what stops the sentence meaning what they meant.

manuscript_tracked-changes.docx — 12 decisions Author’s wording kept in every one
01

Pronoun reference

Copy edit
Original

Knowing that you have something to contribute is not arrogance; that is confidence.

Edit

Knowing that you have something to contribute is not arrogance; it is confidence.

Margin comment

“That” has to reach back over “arrogance” to find what it means, so for a moment the sentence appears to say that arrogance is confidence. “It” attaches straight to “knowing that you have something to contribute,” which is what you mean.

02

Meaning restored

Copy edit
Original

Your needs may sometimes conflict with those you supervise or even those who supervise you.

Edit

Your needs may sometimes conflict with those of the people you supervise, or even with those who supervise you.

Margin comment

As written, your needs conflict with the people rather than with their needs, which is not what the sentence means. “Those of” restores the comparison the sentence is making.

03

One comma

Copy edit
Original

Although we may want to identify with others’ pain, as we naturally should to bring encouragement, this is often a difficult, if not an impossible task, since we are not walking in their shoes.

Edit

Although we may want to identify with others’ pain, as we naturally should to bring encouragement, this is often a difficult, if not an impossible, task, since we are not walking in their shoes.

Margin comment

One comma added. The interruption “if not an impossible” needs closing so that both “difficult” and “impossible” attach cleanly to “task.” Your wording is untouched.

04

One comma

Copy edit
Original

We must ask ourselves has leadership forgotten what it felt like to be an employee?

Edit

We must ask ourselves, has leadership forgotten what it felt like to be an employee?

Margin comment

One comma. The question needs an introduction; without it the two halves run together and the reader has to go back.

05

Dangling modifier

Copy edit
Original

After twenty years in the field, the lessons still surprise me.

Edit

After twenty years in the field, I am still surprised by the lessons.

Margin comment

As written, the lessons have spent twenty years in the field. Attaching the opening phrase to the person it describes fixes it. Your sentence keeps its length, its order and its quiet tone.

06

Parallel structure

Line edit
Original

A good manager listens well, clear communication, and they follow through.

Edit

A good manager listens well, communicates clearly, and follows through.

Margin comment

Three items in three different grammatical shapes, so the reader re-reads to hold all three. Matching them to verbs fixes that. Same three qualities, same plain register you use throughout the chapter.

07

Agreement across a long subject

Copy edit
Original

The pressure of deadlines, shifting priorities, and constant interruption make focus almost impossible.

Edit

The pressure of deadlines, shifting priorities, and constant interruption makes focus almost impossible.

Margin comment

The subject is “the pressure,” not the three items listed inside it, so the verb is singular. One letter. Nothing else touched.

08

Redundancy

Line edit
Original

In my own personal experience, I have personally found that most leaders already know this.

Edit

In my experience, most leaders already know this.

Margin comment

“Own,” “personal” and “personally” each say the same thing once. Cutting two of them speeds the line without softening the claim or flattening the conversational voice you write in.

09

Tense consistency

Copy edit
Original

She walks into the meeting knowing the numbers were wrong, and said nothing.

Edit

She walked into the meeting knowing the numbers were wrong, and said nothing.

Margin comment

The story is told in the past everywhere else; “walks” pulls the reader into the present for one clause and then drops them back. One letter changes and the scene holds.

10

Rhythm

Line edit
Original

The team was tired. The deadline was close. The client was anxious. The plan was failing.

Edit

The team was tired, the deadline close, the client anxious. The plan was failing.

Margin comment

Four identical short sentences flatten into a drumbeat and the last one loses the weight you clearly intended. Compressing the first three lets the fourth land. Every word here is yours — only the punctuation moved.

11

Structure — a note, not an edit

Developmental
Original

Chapter 4 opens with the three-part framework, then tells the story it came from.

Edit

Suggested: open with the story, and let the framework arrive on page three as the thing that story forced you to invent.

Margin comment

This is a structural note, not a line edit — I have changed no wording. Readers accept a framework far more readily once they have watched it earn its keep. Your framework, your story, your decision to take or decline.

12

Left exactly as written

Voice preserved
Original

And that is the whole of it.

Edit

And that is the whole of it.

Margin comment

Flagged by grammar software as a fragment beginning with “And.” Left untouched. It is how you talk, it lands the paragraph, and the rule it supposedly breaks is not a rule. Knowing what not to change is half of what you are paying an editor for.

What this section is proving. Not one of these edits replaces the author’s vocabulary, rhythm or opinion. Each one removes something that stopped the sentence saying what the author already meant — and each arrives with a comment explaining why, so the decision stays yours. This is exactly what lands in your inbox: tracked changes you accept one at a time, never a rewritten file handed back in silence.

About

I edit the books that have to hold up in the room.

Who I am: Adeyanju J. — a Top Rated freelance book editor based in Surulere, Lagos, Nigeria, working with English-language nonfiction authors worldwide. I specialise in developmental, line and copy editing for self-help, business, leadership and coaching books.

Most of my clients are not career writers. They are coaches, consultants, founders, pastors and operators who know their subject cold and have finally written the book — and who now need someone to make the page as convincing as they are in person.

That is a specific job. It is not proofreading, and it is not rewriting you into somebody else. I work in tracked changes with comments that explain the why, because an edit you do not understand is an edit you cannot learn from. You will finish the project a measurably better writer — that is deliberate.

What I will not do: flatten your voice into corporate mush, disappear for two weeks, hand you a file full of silent changes, or tell you a book is ready when it is not.
